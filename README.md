# Installed Base Reliability and Service Cost Forecasting

A four part analysis of how a fleet of industrial machines fails, what servicing it will cost, and how to forecast cost for a new machine platform that has almost no field history. It is built around the problem a semiconductor equipment maker such as KLA faces: tools are sold into fabs and then serviced for years under fixed price contracts, so the company collects a known amount and absorbs whatever failures actually happen.

Everything is validated out of sample. Where a sophisticated method lost to a simple baseline, the notebooks say so and explain why.

## Key results

| Question | Result |
|---|---|
| Can the failure records be trusted? | 743 of 761 failures link to a replacement record. The other 18 share one timestamp at the very start of the failure log, at an hour no other record uses, and were quarantined as a logging artifact rather than turned into invented lifetimes. |
| How do components fail? | All four components **wear out**: Weibull shape β between 1.61 and 1.99, with every 95% CI (Confidence Interval) above 1. The weakest part (comp2) reaches 10% failed by day 30. |
| What drives failure? | A first model found "significant" telemetry drivers. They were leakage: telemetry averaged over the whole year scored early lifetimes with future data. After a strict time split, the only driver that survives is **machine age for comp4**: HR (Hazard Ratio) 1.14 per year, cross validated C index 0.78. |
| What will next quarter cost? | In backtests, simple persistence ("same as last quarter") had the lowest error, WAPE (Weighted Absolute Percentage Error) 9.8%, because this fleet is in a steady state. The renewal simulation reached 13.5% with fleet level misses between −9% and +1%, and it supplies what persistence cannot: calibrated intervals, parts demand, and cost distributions. |
| What about a new platform? | In the first 30 days after launch, a hierarchical Bayesian model has the lowest error of the three methods tested. It beats the fleet average by 16% to 60% from day 30 onward, and after about 60 days the platform's own data is enough on its own. |

## Business context

A service contract is an insurance policy on the vendor's own machines. Three decisions depend on forecasting its cost well:

1. **Contract pricing:** the expected cost the price must cover.
2. **Financial reserves:** a high percentile of cost, so a bad quarter does not become a surprise.
3. **Spare parts stocking:** enough parts near the fab that a failure does not become days of tool downtime.

A fourth problem is the hardest: a **new tool generation** ships with no failure history, yet its contracts must be priced on day one.

## Data

The [Microsoft Azure Predictive Maintenance dataset](https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/arnabbiswas1/microsoft-azure-predictive-maintenance): 100 machines, 4 component types, one year of hourly telemetry, error logs, maintenance records, and failure records. It serves as a public proxy for a tool fleet.

| Dataset | Service analogy |
|---|---|
| machine | tool |
| model (4 types) | tool platform or generation |
| comp1 to comp4 | serviceable modules |
| maintenance record | field service visit that swapped a part |
| failure record | a swap that happened because the part failed |

The dataset is synthetic, and some findings below (the 15 day service schedule, the log start artifact) are properties of how it was generated. The methods for detecting and handling them transfer directly to real service data.

## Part 1: Data foundation

**Goal:** one table where each row is one component lifetime, with its duration and whether it ended in a failure or was censored (swapped preventively, or still running when the data ended).

**What mattered:**

1. **Observation window.** Maintenance records start in mid 2014, but failures are only logged in 2015. A 2014 replacement cannot be classified as failure or preventive, so only lifetimes ending inside the 2015 window are classified.
2. **Linking failures to replacements.** Three passes: exact match, snap within a 12 hour tolerance, quarantine. All 18 quarantined failures sit at 2015-01-02 03:00, the first timestamp of the failure log, while every other record in the dataset is stamped 06:00.
3. **Censoring kept, not discarded.** 3,279 lifetimes: 743 failures, 2,143 preventive swaps, 393 still running. Averaging only failed lifetimes would ignore 77% of the evidence.

**Baseline:** exposure based AFR (Annualized Failure Rate) of 0.98 to 1.93 failures per installed unit per year. The top 10% of machines account for 19.1% of failures, about twice an even share.

## Part 2: Survival modeling

**Goal:** describe how each component ages and what drives failure.

**Methods:** Kaplan Meier curves with left truncation (parts installed before the window only count as at risk from the age observation began), 2 and 3 parameter Weibull fits by maximum likelihood with a machine level cluster bootstrap, and per component Cox PH models.

**Weibull results:**

| Component | β (95% CI) | η, days | B10, days | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| comp1 | 1.86 (1.69 to 2.07) | 147 | 44 | wear out |
| comp2 | 1.61 (1.53 to 1.72) | 120 | 30 | wear out |
| comp3 | 1.96 (1.83 to 2.10) | 174 | 55 | wear out |
| comp4 | 1.99 (1.85 to 2.12) | 149 | 48 | wear out |

B10 is the age by which 10% of parts have failed, the usual anchor for a preventive replacement interval.

**A trap the fitting avoided.** The 3 parameter Weibull adds a failure free period γ. When β < 1 its likelihood grows without limit as γ approaches the earliest failure, so an unguarded fit reports a spurious "infant mortality after a failure free period". An early version did exactly that for comp2 (γ = 25.74 days, which is 0.99 × the first failure at 26 days, with β = 0.92). The final version accepts a 3 parameter fit only if AIC (Akaike Information Criterion) improves, β ≥ 1, and γ is clearly inside its range, and the bootstrap repeats that whole selection on every resample. All four components ended up with the 2 parameter model.

**Drivers, and the leakage that was removed.** The first Cox model used each machine's telemetry averaged over the full year. It found pressure (p = 0.039), voltage (p = 0.011), and rotation (p < 0.001) significant, but failed on held out machines (C index 0.48 for two components, a coin flip). The averages leaked the future into the past, and machines that fail often spend more time in degraded states, so their averages drift *because* they failed.

The fix: usage profiles from January to June only, and models fit only on lifetimes starting from July. After the fix:

| Component | C index, 5 fold grouped cross validation | Verdict | Significant driver |
|---|---|---|---|
| comp1 | 0.593 ± 0.076 | usable (borderline) | none |
| comp2 | 0.507 ± 0.046 | not reliable | none |
| comp3 | 0.544 ± 0.166 | not reliable | none |
| comp4 | 0.779 ± 0.098 | usable | machine age, HR 1.14 per year (1.08 to 1.20) |

No telemetry effect is significant for any component. Part 3 applies machine level adjustments only where the verdict is usable.

## Part 3: Fleet cost forecast

**Goal:** forecast next quarter's failures, parts, and cost with uncertainty, and prove it on quarters already observed.

**Method:** a Monte Carlo renewal simulation. Every installed part has two competing clocks, failure and preventive swap. Whichever rings first ends its life, and the replacement starts at age 0, so a part can fail and be replaced more than once in a quarter.

**The data runs on a 15 day schedule.** 90% of preventive swap intervals and 77% of failure lifetimes are exact multiples of 15 days: field service visits every 15 days, and events are recorded at visits. A smooth Weibull cannot reproduce that. For comp1 it puts 17% of parts failed by day 60, where the data shows 27%. So the swap clock is empirical (Kaplan Meier), and the failure clock is tried both as a Weibull and as an empirical distribution, with the backtest choosing.

**Backtest:** five rolling origins (days 150 to 275), history rebuilt as of each origin so no future data leaks, 90 day horizon, 20 component level forecasts.

| Method | MAE (failures) | Bias | WAPE | FVA vs naive | 80% interval coverage |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Naive, last quarter | 4.35 | +1.55 | 9.8% | 0% | none |
| Constant AFR | 5.47 | +2.80 | 12.3% | −26% | none |
| Weibull, first failure only | 7.54 | +1.95 | 17.0% | −73% | none |
| Simulation, Weibull failure clock | 7.84 | −5.57 | 17.7% | −80% | 40% |
| **Simulation, empirical failure clock** | **5.96** | **−1.65** | **13.5%** | **−37%** | **70%** |

MAE is Mean Absolute Error and FVA is Forecast Value Added.

**Reading it honestly.**
1. The empirical clock cuts MAE by 24% relative to the Weibull clock. That makes the Weibull the right model for *understanding* ageing (Part 2) and the empirical clock the right one for *counting* events on this schedule.
2. Persistence wins because monthly failures are flat: the fleet is in a steady state. The simulation earns its place elsewhere: calibrated intervals, parts and cost from the same run, and sensitivity to changes in age mix, swap policy, or installed base that persistence cannot see.
3. The remaining negative bias is consistent with the independent clocks assumption being violated, for example technicians swapping parts that look close to failing.

**Forward forecast for the quarter after the data ends** (costs are illustrative placeholders, since the dataset has none):

| Decision | Number |
|---|---|
| Expected failures | 165 |
| Expected preventive swaps | 522 |
| Parts to stock per component (95% of quarters covered) | 185 to 200 |
| Expected cost, for pricing | $7.56M |
| P90 cost, for reserves | $7.92M (+$360k, 4.8% above expected) |

Fleet percentiles come from the simulated fleet total, not from summing component percentiles, which would overstate the reserve.

## Part 4: Forecasting a new generation with sparse data

**Goal:** a usable forecast for a platform with almost no field history, that improves as data arrives.

**Method:** a hierarchical Gamma Poisson model. Each component and platform pair has a hazard multiplier relative to the component's Weibull baseline, with exposure measured in cumulative hazard so that platforms with older parts are expected to fail more. The multipliers share a Gamma prior whose mean and spread are learned from the existing platforms, so the amount of borrowing is estimated, not chosen. Conjugacy makes the posterior exact on a grid.

**Experiment:** hold out each platform in turn, learn the hierarchy from the other three, launch the held out platform on day 30, 60, or 90, reveal its data over time, and forecast its failures for the rest of the year with three methods.

| Days after launch | MAE, platform only | MAE, fleet average | MAE, hierarchical | Hierarchical 80% coverage |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 15 | 18.7 | 17.0 | **16.9** | 54% |
| 30 | 13.7 | 15.8 | **13.4** | 65% |
| 60 | **9.2** | 14.2 | 9.8 | 79% |
| 90 | **7.1** | 12.6 | 7.5 | 79% |
| 180 | **2.9** | 7.8 | 3.1 | 92% |

**Reading it:**
1. In the first month the hierarchical model is best, the period when a contract is priced.
2. The fleet average never learns. By day 180 it is off by 2.5 times as much as the hierarchical model.
3. From about day 60, the platform's own data is enough, and shrinkage costs 5% to 7% in MAE. The learned prior is weak (α ≈ 1) because platforms differ sharply: comp3 fails at 2.6 to 3.2 times its baseline on two platforms and essentially never on the other two.
4. Early intervals are too narrow. Failures rise and fall across the calendar for the whole fleet at once, and a short window after launch mostly measures which month it fell in. Pooling across platforms cannot remove a shock they all share.
5. Conclusions do not depend on the hyperprior: MAE at 30 days is 12.1 under all three priors tested.

**In dollars:** comp3 on model3 had zero failures all year. After 30 days with zero failures, pricing a hypothetical 500 tool, three year contract on the fleet average would have overpriced it by $22.8M. The hierarchical estimate cut that to $3.7M and was still moving toward zero as evidence accumulated.

## Limitations

1. The dataset is synthetic, one year long, and has 100 machines and 4 platforms. The hierarchy's spread parameter is estimated from 12 to 16 cells.
2. Costs are placeholders. In practice they come from the parts catalog and labor records.
3. Failure and swap clocks are assumed independent, and the backtest's negative bias suggests they are not entirely.
4. Empirical clocks cannot extrapolate beyond ages seen in the data.
5. The installed base is fixed. A production forecast would add new installations and retirements.

## Next steps

1. **Dependent competing risks,** so that swaps of near failure parts are modeled rather than assumed away.
2. **Component specific prior spread** in the hierarchy, since platforms are near identical for comp1 and comp2 but wildly different for comp3.
3. **Design similarity covariates** (shared subsystems, product family, process node), so a new platform borrows most from its closest relatives.
4. **Time varying telemetry** in the driver models, measured strictly before each event, to test whether sensor data adds early warning once leakage is ruled out.
